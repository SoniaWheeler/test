🧩 Lesson 3 – Designing Data Solutions

Generate, compare and select a suitable design for a data investigation.

🎯 Learning Intention

In this lesson, you will learn how to create different possible designs for a data solution and decide which design best meets the requirements.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
  • identify the requirements of a data investigation
  • generate different possible designs
  • compare designs against the requirements
  • select a preferred design and explain your choice

📖 Theory: What is a Data Solution?

A data solution is a planned way of using data to investigate a question or solve a problem.

A data solution might involve decisions about:

  • what data needs to be collected
  • where the data will come from
  • how the data will be stored
  • how the data will be analysed
  • how the results will be displayed
Important: There is often more than one way to solve a data problem. Before choosing a solution, we can consider several possible designs.

📋 What are Requirements?

Requirements describe what a solution needs to achieve. They help us decide whether a particular design is suitable.

For example, a data investigation might require that the solution:

  • collects enough data to identify a pattern
  • collects the information needed to answer the question
  • uses reliable and appropriate data
  • can be completed within the available time
  • presents the results clearly
Think of requirements as a checklist.
A good design should meet the important requirements of the problem.

🔄 Why Generate Alternative Designs?

Instead of immediately choosing the first idea that comes to mind, a designer can create several possible solutions.

The different designs can then be compared. One design might be faster, another might collect more data, while another might produce results that are easier to understand.

Remember: The preferred design is not simply the one that sounds the most interesting. It should be the design that best meets the requirements.

🔎 Example: Investigating Sleep and Reaction Time

Imagine that we want to investigate this question:

Does the amount of sleep a student gets affect their reaction time?

We could design this investigation in several different ways.

Design A

Survey 30 students about how many hours they slept the previous night. Each student then completes one reaction-time test.

Display the results using a scatter plot.

This design collects data from many students and directly compares sleep with reaction time.

Design B

Survey students and divide them into three groups: less than 6 hours, 6–8 hours and more than 8 hours.

Calculate the average reaction time for each group and display the results using a column graph.

This design makes it easy to compare groups, but grouping the data may hide some differences between individual students.

Design C

Ask five students to record their sleep and reaction time every day for one week.

Compare each student's results over the week.

This design provides repeated measurements, but it involves fewer students and takes longer to complete.

🧠 Think About the Designs

Before continuing, think about the three designs above.

Which design would be easier to complete within one school lesson?

Which design might provide information from the largest number of students?

Which design would provide repeated measurements over several days?
